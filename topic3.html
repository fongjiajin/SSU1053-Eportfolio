01

Depth of Understanding

LU3 introduced me to the seven characteristics of words, and together they reshaped how I think about language. The lecture began with the idea that words are symbols — they stand in for thoughts, objects, and feelings, and they only carry meaning because we agree they do. From there, the lecture showed that words are arbitrary: the sounds and letters we use have no natural connection to what they represent. The word "bunga" does not inherently mean "flower"; it is simply a label we have attached to the concept. The same applies to ambiguous words, which carry more than one meaning and often become the source of unintentional humour — the golf course sign warning that offenders will "have their balls removed" is a perfect example of how a simple statement can slip into comedy.

The lecture then moved into the difference between abstract and concrete nouns. Abstract words like "love," "trust," and "knowledge" cannot be detected by the five senses, while concrete words like "car," "tree," and "book" can. The more specific a term becomes, the more concrete it is: "leisure activities" is abstract, but "playing badminton on Sunday morning" is concrete. This connects directly to the fifth characteristic — words are denotative and connotative. Denotation is the dictionary meaning that stays stable across cultures; connotation is the personal and cultural meaning that shifts depending on experience. The word "home," for example, literally refers to a place of residence, but for many it carries warmth, safety, and belonging.

What struck me most was the sixth and seventh points: meanings are in people, and words are culture-bound. The lecture asked a simple question — "What is happiness for you?" — and showed answers ranging from money and family to peace, art, and fame. The word "happiness" is the same, but its meaning lives inside each person's frame of reference. Culture shapes this even further: the African proverb "It takes a whole village to raise a child" carries a communal worldview that may not translate smoothly into an individualistic culture. Words, I learned, are never neutral — they carry the fingerprints of the people and cultures who use them.

The second half of the lecture shifted from theory to practice, covering how to manage misunderstanding, how to use words of support, how to apologise, and finally how to be assertive. Each of these is grounded in the same principle: because words are ambiguous, symbolic, and culturally loaded, clear and honest communication is a skill that must be learned. Assertiveness, in particular, stood out — the ability to express your needs, feelings, and opinions openly without violating the rights of others. The lecture listed practical strategies: state your viewpoint without hesitation, learn to say no to unreasonable expectations, paraphrase what others have said, use "I" statements, maintain eye contact, and take ownership of your words.

02

My Reaction

I came into this lecture thinking that words were simple — you say what you mean, and the other person either understands or does not. LU3 dismantled that assumption piece by piece, and it was honestly a little unsettling.

The point that hit hardest was "meanings are in people". I had always assumed that if I said something clearly enough, the other person would receive the meaning I intended. The lecture showed me that this is not how communication works. Two people can use the exact same word — "family", "success", "respect" — and mean completely different things, because meaning is filtered through each person's life experiences. When I thought back to arguments I have had with friends, most of them were not about disagreement at all; they were about two people using the same word to mean different things. That realisation reframed conflict entirely for me.

The section on assertiveness was equally confronting. I had always admired assertive people but secretly believed that being assertive meant being aggressive — that I would have to become someone louder and harder to be heard. The lecture separated the two cleanly: assertiveness is about expressing your needs while still respecting the rights of others. The strategy "learn to say no to unreasonable expectations" was the one I had been avoiding my whole life. I say yes to almost everything, then resent the commitments later, because saying no feels like letting people down. Seeing assertiveness framed as a skill rather than a personality trait made me feel like it was something I could actually practise.

Finally, the lecture's treatment of apologies made me pause. The slides pointed out that a real apology requires an explicit admission of error, an honest acknowledgment that you were wrong, and a request for forgiveness. I realised that many of my past "apologies" were not apologies at all — they were explanations, deflections, or thinly veiled justifications. The quote from the lecture stuck with me: "No one in history has ever choked to death from swallowing his pride." It was funny, but it also stung, because I knew exactly which situations it applied to.

03

Practice / Application

Since LU3, I have started applying several ideas from the lecture in my daily life, and a few of them have already changed how I handle conversations.

The most impactful habit has been paraphrasing before responding. Instead of jumping straight into my own opinion, I now try to summarise what the other person said first: "So what you're saying is...". This does two things — it confirms I heard them correctly, and it gives them a chance to correct me if I did not. Since I started doing this, I have noticed fewer misunderstandings in group discussions. When someone says "that's not what I meant," I catch it earlier, before the conversation has already gone off track.

Second, I am practising being specific rather than abstract. The lecture showed that abstract words like "soon," "better," and "more" are vague and often lead to confusion, while concrete words are clear and actionable. In group projects, I used to say things like "we should improve this part" — which everyone agreed with but no one acted on. Now I try to be concrete: "we should shorten this paragraph from four sentences to two." The difference in how people respond is striking. Concrete language moves conversations forward; abstract language keeps them circling.

Third, I am working on saying no without guilt. This has been the hardest one, because it goes against years of habit. The lecture reminded me that saying yes to every request means saying no to something else — usually my own rest, study time, or peace of mind. I have started using a small technique: instead of saying "no" outright, I say "I can't take that on right now, but I could help next week" or "I'm not the best person for this, but I can suggest someone who is." This keeps me honest without closing the relationship. It is still uncomfortable, but it is getting easier with practice.

Finally, I am being more careful with how I apologise. The lecture's point about explicit admission of error changed something in me. Now when I apologise, I try to name exactly what I did wrong, without the word "but" sneaking in after it. "I'm sorry I forgot to reply to your message" is very different from "I'm sorry, but I was really busy." The first one takes responsibility; the second one takes it back. It is a small change, but I can feel the difference in how people respond.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Understanding verbal communication skills gives me advantages that extend far beyond this course.

In my future career, most of my work will involve communication — emails, meetings, reports, presentations, and negotiations. Knowing that words are arbitrary, ambiguous, and culture-bound means I will treat language as a tool that needs to be used carefully, not casually. When I write an email, I will avoid abstract language and vague deadlines; when I give feedback, I will separate the behaviour from the person; when I disagree, I will express my view without dismissing theirs. The assertive communication strategies from the lecture — stating my viewpoint without hesitation, using "I" statements, and taking ownership — will be especially useful in professional settings where unclear communication can cost time, trust, and money.

In my personal relationships, the principle that meanings are in people will help me avoid unnecessary conflict. When someone reacts strongly to something I said, instead of assuming they are being unreasonable, I will ask myself: what did they hear? Their reaction makes sense from inside their frame of reference, even if it did not come from my words alone. This perspective keeps me curious rather than defensive, and it turns disagreements into opportunities to understand someone better, not to win an argument.

Long term, I believe the lecture's closing quote captures the whole lesson: "Words are free. It's how you use them that may cost you." Words do not cost money, but they can cost relationships, opportunities, and trust if they are used carelessly. The lecture made me aware of just how much responsibility comes with something as ordinary as speaking. Going forward, I want to treat language with the same care I would treat any other tool — checking that it is clear, honest, specific, and kind before I use it.

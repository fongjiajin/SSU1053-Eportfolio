LU 5 / Week 5

Mindful Listening

Reflection · SSU1053 e-Portfolio
01

Depth of Understanding

LU5 defined listening as a physical and psychological process that involves acquiring, assigning meaning to, and responding to symbolic messages from others. The lecture made a distinction I had never fully appreciated: hearing is a physiological activity — sound waves hitting the eardrums — while listening is an active, complex process. The Chinese character for listening (聽) illustrated this beautifully: it contains the radicals for ear, eyes, and heart. To truly listen, you need to see, hear, and care — all at once.

The lecture then broke listening down into five active steps that build on each other. Receiving is the intentional focus on a speaker's message, filtering out other stimuli. Understanding requires decoding the speaker's words and nonverbal cues, then interpreting them through your own knowledge, culture, beliefs, and past experience. Remembering moves information into immediate, short-term, or long-term memory. Evaluating judges the content of the message or the character of the speaker. And Responding — the final stage — requires both internal emotional reaction and external verbal or nonverbal feedback.

Then came the obstacles. External obstacles include message overload, message complexity, and noise. Internal obstacles include preoccupation, prejudgment, reacting to emotionally loaded language, lack of effort, and failure to adapt listening styles. The lecture also named five unproductive listening habits: pseudo-listening (pretending to listen), monopolizing (steering everything back to yourself), selective listening (hearing only what interests you), defensive listening (taking innocent comments as personal attacks), and ambushing (listening carefully only to gather ammunition).

The lecture closed with something I found genuinely useful: the Stop–Notice–Suspend framework. Stop thinking about what you are going to say next. Notice your own thoughts and feelings, not just the speaker's words. Suspend judgment briefly, and listen carefully before deciding whether you agree or disagree. It also distinguished literal listening (focusing on the exact words) from listening for information (organising and recalling) and listening to support others (paraphrasing, using minimal encouragers, asking questions, and expressing support). Each serves a different purpose — and none of them is automatic.

02

My Reaction

I came into LU5 assuming I already knew how to listen. After all, I listen all day — in lectures, in conversations, in meetings. But the listening behaviour self-assessment in the lecture was genuinely confronting. When I read through the statements, I found myself ticking "frequently" on almost every one of the unproductive behaviours.

The one that hit hardest was pseudo-listening. I realised I do this constantly — nodding, maintaining eye contact, making small sounds of acknowledgment, while my mind is somewhere entirely different, planning my reply or worrying about a deadline. The lecture made me see that this is not a harmless quirk. It is a quiet form of disrespect, because the other person believes they are being heard and adjusts what they say accordingly. When they later discover I did not really absorb any of it, the trust takes a hit.

Defensive listening was another uncomfortable mirror. I have a habit of hearing neutral comments as criticisms — someone says "you seem tired today" and I immediately hear "you look terrible." The lecture named this pattern so precisely that I could not avoid recognising it. It explained so many small conflicts in my life that I had always blamed on the other person's "tone."

And ambushing made me pause. The lecture described it as listening carefully only to collect information that can be used against the speaker. I recognised this in myself during group discussions, where I sometimes listen to a teammate's idea while silently cataloguing every flaw I could use to one-up them. It is a competitive way of listening, not a cooperative one. The lecture made me ask a hard question: how often have I been present in a conversation, and how often have I just been waiting for my turn to speak?

03

Practice / Application

Since LU5, I have started treating listening as a skill that needs deliberate practice rather than a natural talent I either have or do not have.

The first practice is the Stop–Notice–Suspend framework. Before an important conversation — especially one where I know I will want to jump in and argue — I pause for a second and remind myself: stop planning your reply, notice what the other person is actually saying, and suspend judgment until they are done. It sounds simple, but the difference in how the conversation unfolds is enormous. I notice more details. I catch moments where my automatic interpretation does not match what the speaker actually meant. I interrupt less. The conversation feels less like a competition and more like an exchange.

The second practice is paraphrasing before responding. The lecture listed paraphrase as one of the key tools for supportive listening. Instead of jumping directly into my opinion, I now try to summarise what I heard: "So you're saying that...?" or "Let me make sure I've got this — you feel...". This does two things: it confirms my understanding, and it gives the other person a chance to correct me if I have misread them. In conflicts especially, this small habit has reduced misunderstanding dramatically. Many of my past conflicts were not real disagreements — they were two people who had each misheard the other.

The third practice is using minimal encouragers. The lecture mentioned small verbal cues — "mm-hmm," "I see," "go on" — as a way of signalling to the speaker that I am present and following. I used to think these were superficial, but I now see them as essential feedback. When someone is opening up about something difficult, the absence of these small cues makes the silence feel cold and evaluative. A simple "I hear you" can keep the other person talking through something they might otherwise have closed off.

Finally, I am trying to notice my own selective listening. When someone brings up a topic I find boring or uncomfortable, my instinct is to tune out. Now I try to catch that moment — to notice the impulse to check my phone, or to steer the conversation elsewhere — and instead stay with it for a few more seconds. It is uncomfortable, but it has taught me that the parts of a conversation I want to skip are often the parts that matter most.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Of all the topics in this course so far, mindful listening feels like the one with the widest reach — because almost every part of life depends on it.

In my future career, listening will be the difference between being a colleague people trust and being a colleague people avoid. The lecture's list of benefits — enhanced productivity, improved relations, avoided conflict, better negotiation skills, and simply standing out — reads like a description of what makes someone valuable in any workplace. I will need to listen carefully in meetings to understand what is really being asked, in interviews to pick up on what the interviewer actually values, and in client conversations to hear the problem behind the problem. I will also need to recognise my own internal obstacles — prejudgment, preoccupation, and defensiveness — and manage them before they distort what I hear.

In my personal relationships, listening is the invisible foundation. Almost every relationship I value has been shaped by moments where someone listened to me properly — not just heard my words, but really took them in. The lecture made me realise I want to be that person for others. When a friend is upset, I now try not to jump straight to solutions. When a family member is telling a long story, I try to stay with it instead of checking my phone. These small choices are what trust is built from. The lecture's quotation captures it perfectly: "Every good conversation starts with good listening."

Long term, I hope this lecture marks a shift from passive hearing to mindful listening — the kind that focuses attention on the present moment with alertness, care, and vigilance. That is not a skill I will master quickly, but the fact that the lecture framed it as learnable, rather than innate, gives me something to work toward. Listening well is not a talent I either have or do not have. It is a practice I can return to, conversation after conversation, and slowly get better at.

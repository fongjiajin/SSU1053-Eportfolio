Introduction to Communication

Reflection · SSU1053 e-Portfolio
01

Depth of Understanding

Before this lecture, I thought communication simply meant talking or writing to someone. LU1 completely changed that view. The lecture defined communication as a dynamic, ongoing process — not a single act, but a continuous cycle in which people observe, interpret, and adjust their behaviour toward one another. Communication is described as an action on others, an interaction with others, and a reaction to others (McQuail & Windahl, 1993), and this three-part definition helped me see it as something active rather than passive.

The lecture introduced the communication process model: a sender encodes a message, the message travels through a channel, the receiver decodes it, and feedback flows back to the sender. This model showed me that meaning is never transferred directly — it must be encoded and decoded, and misunderstanding happens when sender and receiver do not share at least some common code.

I also learned about the seven principles of communication: it is a dynamic process, symbolic, systematic, involves making inferences, is self-reflective, has consequences, and is complex. Among them, the idea that communication is symbolic stood out — the words, gestures, and images we use are arbitrary and subjective, which means the same message can be interpreted very differently by different people. The lecture also introduced six types of communication: intrapersonal, interpersonal, mediated interpersonal, small group, organizational, and public communication. Seeing these types laid out side by side made me realise that communication operates on many different levels, from the silent conversations inside my own head to the formal interactions of a workplace.

02

My Reaction

I will be honest — when I first saw the topic title “Introduction to Communication,” I assumed it would be a simple recap of things I already knew. After all, I have been communicating my whole life. But the lecture surprised me in two ways.

First, I had never thought about the intrapersonal type of communication before. I always imagined communication as something that happens between people. The idea that I communicate with myself — through reflection, self-talk, or even daydreaming — made me pause. It explained why my thoughts often feel like a conversation I am having with myself, and why the way I talk to myself affects my mood so strongly.

Second, the principle that communication is self-reflective hit close to home. I realised that in the middle of a conversation, I often evaluate myself — “Did that sound rude?”, “Should I have said that?” — and that self-evaluation changes how I continue the conversation. I had never labelled this as part of communication before; I just thought of it as overthinking. Now I see it as an essential feature of being a communicator.

The lecture also reminded me that communication always has consequences — even silence is a message. This was uncomfortable to accept, because I know I have used silence as a way to avoid difficult conversations, not realising that it was still sending a message I did not intend.

03

Practice / Application

From this lecture, I have started applying three things in my daily life.

First, I now pause before I reply. When I remind myself that communication is a process of encoding and decoding, I stop assuming that the other person understood what I meant. I try to phrase my message more clearly and, if needed, ask the other person to repeat what they heard. This small habit has already reduced misunderstandings with my coursemates during group work.

Second, I try to be more mindful of my nonverbal signals. Since the lecture stressed that gestures, facial expressions, and even clothing carry meaning, I started noticing how my posture and tone shift when I am tired or frustrated. During a recent study group, I realised that my arms were crossed even though I was listening carefully — my friend later told me she thought I was disagreeing. Now I deliberately keep my body language open to match my actual intentions.

Finally, I am practising giving feedback. Instead of ending a conversation with a simple “okay,” I try to respond with something specific — a follow-up question, a short summary, or a small reaction — so the other person knows their message was received. This has made my conversations feel more complete.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Understanding communication as a dynamic, symbolic, and systematic process gives me real advantages in both my personal and professional life.

In my future career, I know I will need to communicate with colleagues, clients, and superiors across different channels — emails, meetings, presentations, and casual chats. Knowing that every message is encoded and decoded means I will take extra care to write clearly, confirm understanding, and ask for feedback rather than assuming everyone interprets things the same way. The lecture also taught me that communication is systematic — the setting, timing, and number of people all shape the meaning of a message. This reminds me to choose the right time and channel for important conversations.

In my personal and social life, the principle that communication is self-reflective will help me be more aware of my own behaviour during conversations. Instead of reacting automatically, I can step back, notice how I am coming across, and adjust if needed. This will help me build deeper relationships and avoid unnecessary conflict.

Long term, I hope this lecture marks the beginning of a more thoughtful way of communicating — one that is deliberate, empathetic, and aware of the consequences every message carries.

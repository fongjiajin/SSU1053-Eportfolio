LU 7 / Week 7

Small Group Communication

Reflection · SSU1053 e-Portfolio
01

Depth of Understanding

LU7 introduced the concept of group communication, defining it as the interaction of three or more interdependent members working to achieve a common goal. The lecture broke this down into five core components: members (at least three, though satisfaction and commitment can decrease as the group grows larger), interaction (using verbal and nonverbal messages to establish relationships), goals (the purpose toward which the group is directed), work (the physical or mental effort exerted to accomplish something), and interdependence (the reality that a group is influenced by the actions of its members).

I also learned about the communication process within groups, which includes the basic elements of members, messages, channels, feedback, context, and noise. The lecture then detailed the different group roles. Roles are patterns of behavior expected of group members, and they can be formal (assigned titles) or informal (emergent behaviors). The three main categories of roles are task roles (focused on completing the group's objectives, such as leader, initiator, or devil's advocate), maintenance roles (focused on group harmony, such as supporters, harmonizers, and gatekeepers), and disruptive roles (self-serving behaviors that hinder progress, such as stage hogs, isolates, or cynics).

Finally, the lecture discussed how to succeed in groups and the concept of group dialectics — the competing and contradictory components of group work. Managing group dialectics requires taking a "both and" approach rather than an "either or" approach to tensions, specifically balancing individual goals with group goals, conflict with cohesion, and conforming with nonconforming.

02

My Reaction

I came into LU7 thinking that group work was just a necessary evil of university life. I had experienced group projects before, and my memories were mostly of scheduling conflicts and uneven workloads. I thought of groups as simply individuals doing their assigned parts. The lecture completely dismantled this view by showing me that a group is a complex system of interdependence and shared meaning.

The section on roles was the most eye-opening. I realized that in past group projects, I had naturally gravitated toward the task roles — specifically the secretary or information giver. I was good at organizing and documenting, but I rarely stepped into maintenance roles like the harmonizer or supporter. When tension arose, I would withdraw. The lecture made me see that without maintenance roles, a group might finish the task but the members would leave feeling exhausted or resentful. I also recognized the disruptive roles in others (and honestly, sometimes in myself when I was stressed and turned into a "cynic"). This new vocabulary gave me a way to understand what actually goes wrong in group work.

The concept of group dialectics also hit close to home. I have always struggled with the tension between individual goals and group goals. I often put my own need for a good grade above the group's social cohesion, which can make me push too hard or feel frustrated when others don't care as much. The lecture's advice to hold two opposite truths at the same time — that my individual goals and the group's goals can both matter — gave me a more mature way to navigate this.

03

Practice / Application

Since LU7, I have been much more intentional about how I show up in group settings, especially in my university project groups.

First, I am practicing stepping into maintenance roles. Previously, I would only focus on getting the work done. Now, I actively try to be a supporter and a harmonizer. When I notice a teammate is stressed, I ask how they are doing. When two members disagree, I try to relieve tension with a light joke or by summarizing both sides fairly. This has made the group environment much more comfortable, and ironically, we actually get more done because people aren't afraid to speak up.

Second, I am more aware of disruptive roles — both in myself and others. When I feel myself becoming a blocker (rejecting new ideas simply because they are different) or a cynic (pessimistic about our chances), I force myself to pause and rephrase my concern as a constructive question. When a teammate becomes a stage hog, instead of getting annoyed, I now use the gatekeeper role: "That's a great point, let's hear what Sarah thinks too." The lecture gave me the tools to steer the group back toward balance without creating conflict.

Finally, I am applying the concept of group dialectics. Instead of fighting against the tension between conformity and nonconformity, I try to embrace it. I respect the group's norms and decisions, but I still voice my unique perspective when I feel it is necessary. I no longer view disagreements as a sign that our group is broken; I view them as a necessary part of reaching a better decision.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Understanding small group communication will benefit me long after I finish university, because almost everything in adult life happens in groups.

In my future career, I will almost certainly be part of cross-functional teams, committees, or project groups. Knowing how to identify and balance task roles, maintenance roles, and disruptive roles will make me a valuable team member and, eventually, a better leader. When I lead a group, I will know to look out for the people who are withdrawn (isolates) or overpowering (stage hogs). I will also know how to resolve the dialectic tensions by encouraging both harmony and healthy disagreement.

In my personal life, this knowledge applies to decision-making with family and friends. Whenever a group of us tries to plan a trip or resolve a problem, the same dynamics apply. I now see that group communication is not just a professional skill; it is a life skill. Knowing how to facilitate a group, how to balance individual needs with collective goals, and how to handle conflicts constructively will help me build stronger, more effective relationships everywhere I go.

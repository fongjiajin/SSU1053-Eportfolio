LU 6 / Week 6

Foundations of Interpersonal Communication

Reflection · SSU1053 e-Portfolio
01

Depth of Understanding

LU6 opened with the definition of interpersonal relationships — the connections and interactions between two or more people, ranging from casual acquaintances to deep, long-term bonds like friendships, family ties, and romantic relationships. The lecture was honest about both sides: relationships lessen loneliness, build self-knowledge and self-esteem, enhance physical and emotional health, and secure intellectual and emotional stimulation — but they also put us under pressure, expose us to vulnerability, increase our obligations, and sometimes hurt us. This balanced view was refreshing. Most textbooks only celebrate relationships; LU6 acknowledged that they cost something too.

The heart of the lecture was the six-stage model of relationships. Stage one, contact, has a perceptual phase (seeing what someone looks like) and an interactional phase (the first "hello"). Stage two, involvement, is where mutuality develops — experimenting, testing, spending more time together, sharing feelings. Stage three, intimacy, is where self-disclosure deepens and the two people become a unit, a recognisable pair. Stage four, deterioration, begins when the relationship weakens — first internally as personal dissatisfaction, then interpersonally as communication patterns shift and withdrawal increases. Stage five, repair, is the pause where people try to fix things — first internally by analysing what went wrong, then interpersonally by discussing the problems directly. And stage six, dissolution, is the breaking of bonds, both interpersonally (living separate lives) and socially (the relationship becomes public, like a divorce).

The lecture then moved through different types of relationships. Friendship was defined as a mutually productive relationship characterised by mutual positive regard, with three types — reciprocity (equal), receptivity (unequal but satisfactory, like student-teacher), and association (transitory). Love relationships were organised through the six Greek types: eros (passionate), ludus (playful, entertainment-focused), storge (affectionate, slow-building from friendship), pragma (practical, traditional), mania (obsessive, possessive), and agape (selfless, compassionate). Family relationships were categorised by two orientations — conformity and conversation — producing four family types: consensual (high on both), protective (high conformity, low conversation), pluralistic (low conformity, high conversation), and laissez-faire (low on both).

The lecture closed with workplace communication and interpersonal conflict. Workplace communication takes four forms — lateral (between equals), upward (subordinate to superior), downward (superior to subordinate), and grapevine (informal). Conflict was defined as occurring when people are interdependent, mutually aware of incompatible goals, and see each other as interfering with those goals. The five conflict styles — competing (I win, you lose), avoiding (I lose, you lose), accommodating (I lose, you win), collaborating (I win, you win), and compromising (both partially win and lose) — were presented alongside strategies for managing conflict, including win-win and win-lose negotiation, force and talk, and face-attacking versus face-enhancing.

02

My Reaction

I came into LU6 with the vague belief that relationships "just happen" — that you meet someone, you click, and either you stay close or you drift apart. The six-stage model dismantled that assumption completely. Relationships are not weather; they are processes with identifiable stages, each one requiring different kinds of communication. Once I saw it laid out, I could not stop applying it to my own life.

The stage I have been thinking about the most is deterioration. The lecture distinguished intrapersonal dissatisfaction (the private, internal feeling that something is off) from interpersonal deterioration (the visible shift in communication patterns — less texting, less disclosure, more withdrawal). I realised I have let relationships drift into deterioration without ever consciously deciding to end them. The first feeling of dissatisfaction was so quiet I barely noticed it, and by the time the relationship was visibly fading, I had already started treating it as inevitable. The lecture made me wonder how many relationships I have lost simply because I did not recognise the stage they were in — and therefore did not try to move to the repair stage.

The section on friendship types also reframed something for me. I had always assumed that a "real" friendship had to be reciprocal — an equal give-and-take. The lecture's idea of receptivity — a friendship where one person gives more than the other, but both parties are still satisfied — was a revelation. Some of the relationships I value most are not equal in the way I used to think they had to be. A mentor who gives me time and guidance without expecting anything in return, a younger friend who I look out for more than they look out for me — these are not "incomplete" friendships. They are a different, equally valid type. That reframing has made me more grateful for what I have, instead of measuring it against an ideal.

Finally, the conflict styles section was a mirror. I am a chronic avoider. I would rather swallow my frustration than risk a confrontation, and I have always told myself that this is because I am "easygoing." The lecture showed me that avoiding is not neutral — it is a style with real consequences. When I avoid, both sides lose (I lose because the issue stays unresolved; they lose because the relationship stays shallow). The lecture also pointed out that many conflicts hidden as content conflicts — about what to do — are actually relationship conflicts — about power, equity, or feeling valued. I have definitely had arguments about dishes and deadlines that were really about something deeper. Recognising this makes me want to name the real issue instead of fighting over its surface.

03

Practice / Application

Since LU6, I have started using the lecture's frameworks as tools for examining my own relationships, and a few specific practices have emerged.

The first is stage awareness. When I feel distant from someone I care about, instead of letting it drift, I now ask myself: which stage are we in? Is this deterioration, and if so, is it intrapersonal (I am quietly dissatisfied) or interpersonal (the communication itself has changed)? Once I can name the stage, I can decide whether to move into repair. Sometimes repair looks like a direct conversation — "I feel like we've been out of touch lately, can we talk?" Other times it looks like a smaller gesture — a text, a shared meal, a deliberate re-investment of time. Either way, naming the stage has given me agency I did not have before.

The second practice is adapting my conflict style. My default is avoiding, so I am deliberately practising collaborating — the win-win style where both people articulate their needs and look for a solution that satisfies both. The lecture's win-win negotiation strategies gave me a concrete script: define the conflict as a mutual problem, pursue joint outcomes, use open and honest communication, avoid threats, and communicate flexibility in my position. These are not things I naturally do, but having them written out as a sequence makes them feel doable. In a recent disagreement with a housemate, I tried opening with "I want us both to be comfortable with this — can we figure out a setup that works for both of us?" The conversation went very differently from my usual avoidant silence.

The third practice is recognising relationship conflicts that hide as content conflicts. When I feel a disproportionate amount of emotion about something small — the way a message was phrased, the tone of a reply, a forgotten favour — I now try to ask myself: what is this really about? Is it about the dishes, or is it about feeling taken for granted? Is it about the late reply, or is it about feeling unimportant? The lecture made me realise that most of the "small" conflicts I have had in relationships were not small at all; they were proxies for deeper relational questions about respect, equity, and care. Now I try to name the real issue instead of fighting over the surface.

The fourth practice is treating self-disclosure as a tool rather than a risk. The lecture noted that self-disclosure is what deepens intimacy — it is the mechanism through which relationships move from involvement to closeness. I have always been cautious about revealing too much, afraid of burdening others or losing control of my image. The lecture made me see that this caution is not just protecting me; it is also limiting the relationships I can build. So I have started sharing small things — a worry, a memory, a moment of vulnerability — and letting the other person see me more clearly. It is uncomfortable, but it is the only way relationships actually move forward.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Understanding the foundations of interpersonal communication gives me a map I did not have before — a map of where relationships actually are, where they might go, and what I can do to influence them.

In my future career, I will be navigating relationships constantly — with colleagues, supervisors, clients, and the informal networks that make organisations function. The lecture's model of workplace communication (lateral, upward, downward, and grapevine) makes me more aware of how many different kinds of relationships I will have at work, each with its own rules and expectations. The six-stage model also applies to professional relationships: I will need to build contact and involvement before intimacy, and I will sometimes need to recognise when a working relationship has entered deterioration and either repair it or let it go cleanly. Knowing the stages helps me see that professional relationships are not static — they require the same active maintenance as personal ones.

In my personal relationships, the lecture's frameworks will help me be more deliberate about what I want. When I meet someone new, I will be aware that we are moving through stages, and I will notice when the relationship is stalling. When a friendship starts to fade, I will not just let it drift; I will ask what stage we are in and whether repair is possible. And when I am in conflict, I will have language for what is happening — I am avoiding, I am accommodating, I am collaborating. Naming the pattern is the first step to changing it.

Long term, I believe this lecture marks a shift in how I understand relationships themselves. I used to think of relationships as something that either worked or did not work — as a matter of compatibility. The lecture showed me that relationships are built, maintained, damaged, and repaired through communication. They are not weather; they are architecture. And like architecture, they require design, maintenance, and sometimes repair. That is both more demanding and more hopeful than what I used to believe. More demanding, because I cannot just wait for relationships to happen. More hopeful, because if relationships are made through communication, then I have more influence than I thought — not over the other person, but over the choices I make every day that either strengthen the relationship or let it weaken.

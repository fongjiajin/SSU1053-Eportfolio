LU 2 / Week 2

Identity and Perception

Reflection · SSU1053 e-Portfolio
01

Depth of Understanding

LU2 introduced two deeply connected ideas: identity and perception. Identity was defined as the answer to the questions “Who am I?” and “Who are you?” — a person's perception of self within a particular social, geographical, cultural, and political context. What struck me most is the claim that identity is socially constructed: it exists not in objective reality but because humans agree it exists, and it is created, reflected, and maintained through interactions with other people.

The lecture outlined seven characteristics of identity — multifaceted, social and personal, salience and strength, fluid and stable, avowed and ascribed, passing and outing, and performance and expression. Each one revealed how complex the self really is. For example, the same person can be a mother, a boss, a chef, and a friend — all at once — and which identity becomes salient depends on the situation. The distinction between avowal (what we declare about ourselves) and ascription (what others assign to us) helped me see why misunderstandings about identity happen so often.

The lecture then moved into the three fundamental aspects of the self: self-concept, self-awareness, and self-esteem. Carl Rogers' theory of self-concept distinguishes the self-image (how I see myself), the ideal self (how I wish I were), and self-esteem (how much value I place on myself). The Johari Window introduced four areas of self-awareness — Open, Blind, Hidden, and Unknown — and showed that the goal of growth is to enlarge the Open area through feedback and self-disclosure.

Finally, the lecture turned to perception: the process of selecting, organizing, and interpreting people, objects, events, and situations. Perception is influenced by physiological, psychological, and social factors, and it is shaped by common tendencies such as snap judgments, clinging to first impressions, self-serving bias, expectations, focusing on the obvious, and assuming others are like us. The story of the six blind men and the elephant was a perfect illustration: each person saw a different reality because each touched a different part of the truth.

02

My Reaction

I came into this lecture thinking I already understood myself. I know my name, my age, my hometown, my personality — what more is there to know? By the end, I realised that I had been confusing knowing facts about myself with truly understanding my identity.

The idea that my identity is fluid rather than fixed was strangely freeing. I used to think of myself as “a shy person” — as if shyness was a permanent label. The lecture showed me that social identities shift depending on whom I am with and what I am talking about. I am quiet in a large lecture hall, but talkative in a small group of close friends. Both are still me; neither is the “real” me. Realising this made me less worried about being inconsistent.

The Johari Window was uncomfortable but useful. I had never seriously considered that there is a Blind area — parts of me that others see clearly but I cannot see at all. When I thought about feedback I had received in the past — “you seem angry when you're focused” — I realised those were glimpses into my blind spot. I also had to admit that my Hidden area is quite large: there are thoughts and feelings I rarely share with anyone.

The section on perception hit especially hard because I recognised myself in almost every tendency. I make snap judgments, I cling to first impressions, and — most embarrassingly — I am much more forgiving of my own mistakes than of others'. The self-serving bias slide felt like a mirror I did not want to look into, but needed to.

03

Practice / Application

Since LU2, I have started applying several ideas from the lecture in my daily life.

First, I am trying to shrink my Blind area by actively asking for feedback. After a group presentation, I asked a teammate what I could have done better. She told me I speak too quickly when nervous — something I had never noticed. That feedback alone was worth more than hours of self-reflection, because it came from outside my own perspective.

Second, I am practising slowing down my snap judgments. The lecture emphasised that we often form opinions within seconds, based on incomplete information. When I meet someone new, I now try to notice my first impression without immediately accepting it as truth. This has made me less quick to label people as “friendly”, “difficult”, or “boring” — labels that used to close off any possibility of real understanding.

Third, I am working on self-concept improvement, following the lecture's guidelines: making a firm commitment to personal growth, seeking realistic goals, and avoiding self-sabotage. One small habit I have adopted is secure affirmation — I write short “I can” statements before exams or presentations to remind myself of what I have already achieved. It sounds small, but it has noticeably reduced my anxiety.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Understanding identity and perception gives me tools that will serve me far beyond this course.

In my future career, I will work with people from many different backgrounds. Knowing that identity is socially constructed and shaped by culture, geography, and politics will help me avoid the trap of assuming that everyone thinks the way I do. When a colleague behaves in a way I find strange, I will remind myself that their identity and perception have been shaped by experiences I cannot see. This will make me more patient, more curious, and less judgmental.

In my personal relationships, the Johari Window and the principles of perception will help me communicate more openly. By expanding my Open area through self-disclosure, I can build deeper trust with the people around me. By remembering that my perception is only one perspective — like one of the six blind men — I can listen more carefully to how others see the same situation.

Long term, I believe this lecture marks a shift in how I understand myself and others. Identity is not something I simply have; it is something I constantly create through interaction. Perception is not a passive recording of reality; it is an active process I can shape through awareness and reflection. Carrying this awareness into the rest of my life will, I hope, make me a more thoughtful and empathetic person.

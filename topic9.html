LU 9 / Week 9

Conflict Management

Reflection · SSU1053 e-Portfolio
01

Depth of Understanding

LU9 focused on conflict management, defining conflict as a perceived or actual difference between individuals or groups, often characterised by frustration or opposition. The lecture distinguished between functional (constructive) conflict, which supports organizational goals and fosters critical thinking, and dysfunctional (destructive) conflict, which hinders performance.

A major part of the lecture was dedicated to dispelling conflict myths. I learned that conflict is not always destructive; it should not always be avoided; it is not always caused by personal differences (often it is structural); and it cannot simply be "resolved" by an authority figure exerting power. The lecture also broke conflict down into three types: task conflict (disagreements about the work itself), relationship conflict (personal incompatibilities), and process conflict (disagreements about logistics and delegation).

We then explored the role of power in conflict, noting how power imbalances can suppress voices and lead to resentment. To manage this, strategies like empowerment, negotiation, and building relationships are necessary. Finally, the lecture introduced the five conflict management styles (Avoiding, Accommodating, Competing, Compromising, Collaborating) and a comprehensive set of conflict management skills, including active listening, empathy, using "I" statements, and stress management.

02

My Reaction

I used to be terrified of conflict. In my mind, conflict meant raised voices, broken friendships, and stress. Whenever a disagreement arose in a group project, my default reaction was avoidance. I would stay quiet, hope it would blow over, and secretly resent the people involved. LU9 opened my eyes to the fact that my avoidance was actually a form of conflict management — but usually a poor one, because it left the root issue unresolved.

The myth that "conflict is always a result of personal differences" struck a chord with me. I often blamed people for being "difficult" when we argued. But the lecture pointed out that organizational conflict is frequently structural — unclear roles, mismatched goals, or inadequate channels. When I looked back at past arguments, I realized most of them weren't because we hated each other; they were because we were stressed by a lack of clarity.

I was also surprised by the Competing style. The lecture described it as "The Player" — based on power, aggressive, and win-lose. In some contexts, it is necessary (e.g., standing up for your rights), but it is often toxic. I always thought "competing" just meant winning an argument. Now I see how much damage it can do to team morale if it becomes the default style.

03

Practice / Application

Since LU9, I have started deliberately shifting my conflict management style from Avoiding to Collaborating (the "Win-Win" style).

First, I am practicing active listening and empathy during disagreements. Instead of preparing my counter-argument while the other person is speaking, I now try to fully absorb what they are saying. I ask clarifying questions like, "So you're feeling overwhelmed because of the deadline, is that right?" This small shift immediately lowers the tension, because the other person feels heard.

Second, I am strictly using "I" statements. In the past, I used to say "You never help out," which instantly triggers defensiveness. Now, I try to say "I feel frustrated when I don't get any help with the slides," which states the problem without attacking the person's character. It feels a bit awkward at first, but it is incredibly effective at stopping arguments from spiraling.

Finally, I am becoming aware of power dynamics. If I am leading a group, I know that my silence or agreement might be interpreted as final. I now deliberately ask the quieter members for their opinions, empowering them to speak up. If I am on the receiving end of a power imbalance, I remind myself that my feelings and concerns are valid, and I have a right to negotiate.

04

Benefit for Daily Life / Future

Conflict is inevitable. Whether in a family, a team, or a company, humans will always disagree. The true benefit of LU9 is giving me the tools to face conflict with confidence rather than fear.

In my future career, I know I will face task and process conflicts. Instead of avoiding them, I will treat them as opportunities for innovation. When a teammate disagrees with my idea, instead of feeling attacked, I will use the Collaborating style: "That's an interesting perspective. Let's see how we can combine our ideas to get a better outcome." This mindset will make me a valuable team member and, eventually, a respected leader.

In my personal life, this lesson will help me repair and strengthen my relationships. I used to let small annoyances build up until I exploded. Now I know how to address issues early, calmly, and constructively. I no longer view conflict as a sign that a relationship is broken; I view it as a necessary step toward understanding each other better. As the lecture said, "Conflict is a natural and, at times, beneficial element of organizational life."
